# 7.5. Hidden Markov Models

## 7.5.1. Introduction to Hidden Markov Models

Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) are powerful probabilistic models for analyzing sequential data where the underlying states are not directly observable. They have found applications in speech recognition, bioinformatics, natural language processing, financial modeling, and many other fields.

**Intuitive Understanding**: HMMs are like trying to understand the weather patterns in a city where you can't see the actual weather, but you can observe things that depend on the weather. Imagine you're in a room with no windows, but you can see people's behavior: when it's sunny, people wear sunglasses and carry umbrellas less often; when it's rainy, people carry umbrellas and wear raincoats; when it's cloudy, people might carry umbrellas just in case. The weather (hidden states) follows certain patterns - sunny days often follow sunny days, rainy days often follow rainy days, but sometimes the weather changes. HMMs help you figure out what the weather was like each day by observing people's behavior, and they can also help you predict future weather patterns based on what you've observed.

### Problem Setup

Consider a sequence of observations $`X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n`$ where each $`X_t`$ is generated by an underlying hidden state $`Z_t`$. The key assumptions are:

1. **Markov Property**: The hidden states follow a Markov chain: $`P(Z_t \mid Z_{t-1}, Z_{t-2}, \ldots) = P(Z_t \mid Z_{t-1})`$ - like saying today's weather only depends on yesterday's weather, not on the weather from a week ago
2. **Conditional Independence**: Given the hidden state $`Z_t`$, the observation $`X_t`$ is independent of all other observations and states - like saying once you know it's raining, the fact that people are carrying umbrellas doesn't depend on what the weather was like yesterday or what people were doing yesterday
3. **Stationarity**: The transition and emission probabilities are time-invariant - like saying the weather patterns and how people behave in different weather conditions don't change over time

**Intuition**: These assumptions make the problem tractable. The Markov property means we don't need to remember the entire history - just the most recent state. The conditional independence means that once we know the hidden state, the observation is completely determined by that state. The stationarity means the rules don't change over time, so we can learn them from data.

### Mathematical Formulation

The joint probability of observations and hidden states is:

$$ P(X_1, \ldots, X_n, Z_1, \ldots, Z_n) = P(Z_1) \prod_{t=2}^n P(Z_t \mid Z_{t-1}) \prod_{t=1}^n P(X_t \mid Z_t) $$

This factorization reflects the conditional independence structure of the HMM.

**Intuition**: This formula breaks down the probability of everything we observe and everything we don't observe into simple pieces. It's like saying: "The probability of the entire weather sequence and all the people's behavior is the probability of the first day's weather, times the probability of each subsequent day's weather given the previous day, times the probability of people's behavior given each day's weather." This factorization makes the problem much easier to work with.

## 7.5.2. The Dishonest Casino Example

### Problem Description

Consider a dishonest casino that uses two dice:
- **Fair Die**: Each number (1-6) has probability $`1/6`$ - like a normal die where each number is equally likely
- **Loaded Die**: Number 6 has probability $`1/2`$, other numbers have probability $`1/10`$ each - like a die that's rigged to favor the number 6

The casino switches between dice according to a Markov process, but observers only see the roll outcomes.

**Intuition**: This is like a casino that sometimes uses a fair die and sometimes uses a loaded die that heavily favors the number 6. The casino switches between dice based on certain rules (maybe they switch to the loaded die when they're losing money), but you can only see the numbers that come up, not which die is being used. Your goal is to figure out when the casino is using the loaded die by observing the pattern of numbers.

### Mathematical Setup

**Hidden States** $`Z_t \in \{F, L\}`$ (Fair, Loaded):
- Initial distribution: $`\pi = [P(Z_1 = F), P(Z_1 = L)]`$ - like the probability that the casino starts with the fair die vs. the loaded die
- Transition matrix: $`A = \begin{bmatrix} P(F \mid F) & P(L \mid F) \\ P(F \mid L) & P(L \mid L) \end{bmatrix}`$ - like the rules for switching between dice

**Observations** $`X_t \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}`$:
- Emission matrix: $`B = \begin{bmatrix} P(X=1 \mid F) & \cdots & P(X=6 \mid F) \\ P(X=1 \mid L) & \cdots & P(X=6 \mid L) \end{bmatrix}`$ - like the probability of each number coming up with each die

**Intuition**: The transition matrix tells us how the casino switches between dice. Maybe when they're using the fair die, there's a 90% chance they'll keep using it, and a 10% chance they'll switch to the loaded die. When they're using the loaded die, maybe there's an 80% chance they'll keep using it, and a 20% chance they'll switch back to the fair die. The emission matrix tells us what numbers are likely to come up with each die - the fair die gives equal probabilities to all numbers, while the loaded die heavily favors the number 6.

## 7.5.3. Mathematical Foundation

### Joint Probability Distribution

The joint probability of observations $`\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)`$ and hidden states $`\mathbf{Z} = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)`$ is:

$$ P(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Z} \mid \theta) = P(Z_1 \mid \pi) \prod_{t=2}^n P(Z_t \mid Z_{t-1}, A) \prod_{t=1}^n P(X_t \mid Z_t, B) $$

where $`\theta = (\pi, A, B)`$ are the model parameters.

**Intuition**: This formula breaks down the complete probability into three parts: (1) the probability of the first hidden state (which die the casino starts with), (2) the probability of each subsequent hidden state given the previous one (how the casino switches between dice), and (3) the probability of each observation given the hidden state (what numbers come up with each die). This factorization makes it much easier to work with the model.

### Parameter Definitions

1. **Initial Distribution** $`\pi`$: $`\pi_i = P(Z_1 = i)`$ - like the probability that the casino starts with the fair die vs. the loaded die
2. **Transition Matrix** $`A`$: $`A_{ij} = P(Z_t = j \mid Z_{t-1} = i)`$ - like the probability of switching from die i to die j
3. **Emission Matrix** $`B`$: $`B_{ik} = P(X_t = k \mid Z_t = i)`$ - like the probability of rolling number k with die i

**Intuition**: These parameters completely define the HMM. The initial distribution tells us how the process starts, the transition matrix tells us how states change over time, and the emission matrix tells us what observations are likely in each state. Together, they define the complete probabilistic model.

### Marginal Likelihood

The marginal likelihood of observations is:

$$ P(\mathbf{X} \mid \theta) = \sum_{\mathbf{Z}} P(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Z} \mid \theta) $$

This sum has $`m_z^n`$ terms, making direct computation intractable for large $`n`$.

**Intuition**: The marginal likelihood is like asking "how likely are we to see these observations given our model?" We have to sum over all possible hidden state sequences, which becomes computationally impossible as the sequence gets longer. This is why we need clever algorithms like the forward-backward algorithm to compute this efficiently.

## 7.5.4. Forward-Backward Algorithm

### Forward Algorithm

The forward probability $`\alpha_t(i) = P(X_1, \ldots, X_t, Z_t = i \mid \theta)`$ is computed recursively:

$$ \alpha_t(i) = P(X_t \mid Z_t = i) \sum_{j=1}^{m_z} \alpha_{t-1}(j) P(Z_t = i \mid Z_{t-1} = j) $$

**Initialization**: $`\alpha_1(i) = \pi_i P(X_1 \mid Z_1 = i)`$

**Recursion**: $`\alpha_t(i) = B_{i,X_t} \sum_{j=1}^{m_z} \alpha_{t-1}(j) A_{ji}`$

**Intuition**: The forward algorithm is like computing the probability of being in each state at each time step, given all the observations up to that point. It's like saying "given that I've seen the first t rolls, what's the probability that the casino is currently using the fair die vs. the loaded die?" The algorithm builds this up step by step, using the fact that the probability of being in state i at time t depends on the probabilities of being in all states at time t-1 and the transition and emission probabilities.

### Backward Algorithm

The backward probability $`\beta_t(i) = P(X_{t+1}, \ldots, X_n \mid Z_t = i, \theta)`$ is computed recursively:

$$ \beta_t(i) = \sum_{j=1}^{m_z} P(Z_{t+1} = j \mid Z_t = i) P(X_{t+1} \mid Z_{t+1} = j) \beta_{t+1}(j) $$

**Initialization**: $`\beta_n(i) = 1`$

**Recursion**: $`\beta_t(i) = \sum_{j=1}^{m_z} A_{ij} B_{j,X_{t+1}} \beta_{t+1}(j)`$

**Intuition**: The backward algorithm is like computing the probability of future observations given the current state. It's like saying "if I know the casino is currently using the fair die, what's the probability of seeing the remaining rolls?" This is useful for computing the complete likelihood and for the backward pass in the forward-backward algorithm.

## 7.5.5. Viterbi Algorithm

### Problem Statement

Find the most likely sequence of hidden states given observations:

$$ \mathbf{Z}^* = \arg\max_{\mathbf{Z}} P(\mathbf{Z} \mid \mathbf{X}, \theta) $$

**Intuition**: The Viterbi algorithm is like finding the single most likely explanation for the observations. Instead of computing probabilities for all possible state sequences, it finds the one sequence that is most likely to have generated the observations. It's like asking "what's the most likely sequence of dice that the casino used to generate these rolls?"

### Dynamic Programming Solution

Define $`\delta_t(i) = \max_{Z_1, \ldots, Z_{t-1}} P(Z_1, \ldots, Z_{t-1}, Z_t = i, X_1, \ldots, X_t \mid \theta)`$

**Initialization**: $`\delta_1(i) = \pi_i P(X_1 \mid Z_1 = i)`$

**Recursion**: $`\delta_t(i) = P(X_t \mid Z_t = i) \max_j [\delta_{t-1}(j) P(Z_t = i \mid Z_{t-1} = j)]`$

**Backtracking**: Store $`\psi_t(i) = \arg\max_j [\delta_{t-1}(j) P(Z_t = i \mid Z_{t-1} = j)]`$

**Intuition**: The Viterbi algorithm uses dynamic programming to find the optimal path through the state space. At each time step, it computes the maximum probability of being in each state, given all observations up to that point. The key insight is that the optimal path to state i at time t must go through the optimal path to some state j at time t-1, so we can build the solution incrementally.

## 7.5.6. Baum-Welch Algorithm (EM for HMM)

### E-step: Compute Expectations

Compute the following quantities:

1. **State probabilities**: $`\gamma_t(i) = P(Z_t = i \mid \mathbf{X}, \theta)`$ - like the probability that the casino was using die i at time t
2. **Transition probabilities**: $`\xi_t(i,j) = P(Z_t = i, Z_{t+1} = j \mid \mathbf{X}, \theta)`$ - like the probability that the casino switched from die i to die j at time t

**Intuition**: The E-step is like making educated guesses about the hidden states given the observations and current parameter estimates. We compute the probability of being in each state at each time, and the probability of transitioning between states at each time. These probabilities are used to update our parameter estimates in the M-step.

### M-step: Update Parameters

**Initial distribution**:
$$ \pi_i = \gamma_1(i) $$

**Transition matrix**:
$$ A_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n-1} \xi_t(i,j)}{\sum_{t=1}^{n-1} \gamma_t(i)} $$

**Emission matrix**:
$$ B_{ik} = \frac{\sum_{t: X_t = k} \gamma_t(i)}{\sum_{t=1}^n \gamma_t(i)} $$

**Intuition**: The M-step is like updating our parameter estimates based on our current understanding of the hidden states. The initial distribution is updated based on our best guess of the first state. The transition matrix is updated based on how often we think transitions occurred. The emission matrix is updated based on how often we think each state generated each observation.

The Baum-Welch algorithm implementation provides the Expectation-Maximization (EM) approach for HMM parameter estimation. The `BaumWelchHMM` class implements the complete EM algorithm with E-step and M-step iterations.

**Key Functions:**
- `BaumWelchHMM.__init__()`: Initialize with number of states and observations - like setting up the Baum-Welch learning system
- `BaumWelchHMM.initialize_parameters()`: Initialize parameters randomly - like starting with random guesses about the casino's behavior
- `BaumWelchHMM.fit()`: Fit HMM using Baum-Welch algorithm with convergence checking - like learning the casino's rules from the observed data
- `BaumWelchHMM._e_step()`: E-step: Compute gamma and xi using forward-backward - like making educated guesses about hidden states
- `BaumWelchHMM._m_step()`: M-step: Update parameters using computed expectations - like refining parameter estimates based on current understanding
- `demonstrate_baum_welch()`: Complete demonstration with parameter fitting - like showing how the Baum-Welch algorithm learns from data

The Baum-Welch algorithm is essential for learning HMM parameters from data and provides the foundation for unsupervised learning in sequential data.
